## A real risk, above all for the crew

<Term id="turbulence">Turbulence</Term> is air movement that usually cannot be seen and can appear without warning, even in a clear sky. According to the FAA, of the **207 serious turbulence injuries** recorded on US airline flights since 2009, **166 were suffered by crew members**. The reason is simple: the crew are standing and working when the passengers are already seated.

## Recognising it by its effects

The FAA's AIM also describes turbulence intensity by what is felt in the cabin:

| Intensity | In the cabin |
|---|---|
| **Light** | Occupants feel a slight strain against their seat belts; unsecured objects move slightly; service and walking are possible with little or no difficulty |
| **Moderate** | Definite strain against seat belts; unsecured objects are dislodged; service and walking are difficult |
| **Severe** | Occupants are forced violently against their seat belts; unsecured objects are tossed about; service and walking are impossible |

## Management principles

- **Seat belt fastened whenever seated**, not just when the sign is on. The FAA recommends this to all passengers.
- **Advance information:** in the briefing, the flight deck shares where and when turbulence is expected, and the service is planned around it.
- **With the seat-belt sign on:** an announcement to passengers, seat-belt checks if it is safe to do them, and galleys and carts secured.
- **If turbulence is severe or arrives without warning:** the crew sit down and strap in **immediately**, in the nearest free seat if necessary, and brake the carts. No standing up to keep checking seat belts.
- **Two-way communication:** the cabin crew tell the <Term id="flight-deck" /> what they are experiencing and about anyone injured.
