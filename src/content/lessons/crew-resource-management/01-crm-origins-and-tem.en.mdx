## Accidents with aircraft that worked

In the late 1970s, several major accidents had something in common: the aircraft worked, the pilots could fly and yet the **crew as a team** failed.

- **Tenerife, 27 March 1977**: two Boeing 747s collided on the runway at Los Rodeos in fog; 583 people died. Factors included ambiguous communication with ATC and crew doubts that did not prevail over the captain's decision to take off.
- **Portland, December 1978**: a DC-8 ran out of fuel while the crew focused on a landing gear problem; the other crew members' warnings about the fuel were not firm enough.

In 1979 NASA held a workshop on resource management on the flight deck. CRM was born from it, first as *cockpit* and later as **crew** resource management, including the whole crew: <Term id="crm" />.

## What CRM is today

CRM is the **effective use of all available resources**: people (pilots, cabin crew, ATC, maintenance, dispatch), equipment and information, so that the flight is safe and efficient. In the EU CRM training is mandatory in initial training, on each type and on a recurrent basis, and part of it is done together with the cabin crew.

It covers, among other topics: communication, leadership and teamwork, situational awareness, decision making, workload, human factors (Module 12) and automation.

## Threat and error management

The <Term id="tem" /> model starts from one idea: threats and errors are **normal** on any flight; what matters is managing them.

| Element | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Threat** | Something outside the crew that increases complexity | Thunderstorms, short runway, runway change, aircraft with MEL items, rushed ATC, fatigue |
| **Error** | A crew action or inaction that departs from what was intended | Wrong level selected, missed checklist, wrong readback |
| <Term id="undesired-aircraft-state" /> | Position, speed or configuration that reduces margins | High and fast on final, off clearance |

The typical chain is **threat → error → undesired state → event**. Each link is a chance to break it.

### Countermeasures

- **Before**: briefings that identify the flight's threats and how they will be managed.
- **During**: monitoring and cross-checking to catch errors early.
- **If an undesired state appears**: recognise it and recover the aircraft, **not** keep fixing the error that caused it. The classic example: with an unstable approach, the priority is the go-around, not to keep trying to get down.

<Callout type="info" label="Observing normal operations">
Programmes such as LOSA observe normal flights, with no consequences for the crew, to learn which threats and errors really occur and how they are managed.
</Callout>
