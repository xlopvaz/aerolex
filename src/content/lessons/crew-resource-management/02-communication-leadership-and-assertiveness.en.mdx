## Briefings

A good briefing is not reciting the chart: it is **sharing the plan** and its threats so that both pilots have the same mental model. A useful one:

- is **short** and focused on what matters today;
- identifies the **threats** (weather, runway, NOTAMs, fatigue…) and how they will be managed;
- says what will be done if something goes wrong (go-around, diversion);
- ends with a clear **invitation**: "Any questions or anything to add?".

The briefing between pilots and **cabin crew** before the flight is just as important: flight time, expected turbulence, special situations, emergency signals.

## Closed-loop communication

Every important message is **confirmed**:

> **PF**: "Set flight level two four zero."
> **PM**: "Flight level two four zero set."
> **PF**: "Checked."

The sender knows the message arrived and the receiver shows it was understood. The same as the readback with ATC (Module 13).

## The authority gradient

The <Term id="authority-gradient" /> is the difference in perceived authority between crew members:

| Gradient | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Too steep** | The first officer or cabin crew dare not speak; the captain decides alone |
| **Too flat** | Nobody decides; weak leadership |
| **Appropriate** | The captain leads and everyone feels able to speak up |

The captain sets the tone from the start, for example by inviting the crew to say anything they are unhappy with.

## Graded assertiveness

Voicing a concern firmly, without aggression, is a skill that is trained. A widely used model is **PACE**, which escalates:

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| **P**robe | "Have you noticed we're 20 knots fast?" |
| **A**lert | "We're 20 knots fast at 1,500 ft; we won't be stabilised." |
| **C**hallenge | "We need to go around now." |
| **E**mergency | "Go around" and, if there is no response and the risk is immediate, act according to the operator's procedure |

Another technique is to use agreed key words that everyone understands as a signal to stop.

## Leadership and followership

The captain has final responsibility, but a good leader **listens**, **delegates**, **keeps the big picture** and decides. The rest of the crew also has an active role: supporting, monitoring and speaking up when something does not fit.

<Callout type="tip" label="Say it as you'd see it in the report">
If a concern would deserve a place in an incident report, it deserves to be said now, and clearly.
</Callout>
