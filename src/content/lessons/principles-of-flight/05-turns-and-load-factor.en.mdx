## What makes an aeroplane turn

When the aircraft banks, the <Term id="lift" /> tilts with the wings. It can be split into two parts:

- a **vertical component**, which supports the <Term id="weight" />;
- a **horizontal component**, which pulls the aircraft towards the centre of the turn: this is what makes it turn.

Because only part of the lift still supports the weight, to turn **without losing height** the pilot must increase total lift by pulling back to raise the angle of attack (and adding power if needed).

## Load factor

<Term id="load-factor">Load factor</Term> (n) is the ratio of lift to weight. In straight-and-level flight it is 1 g. In a **level, coordinated turn**:

**n = 1 / cos(bank angle)**

| Bank | Load factor | Stall speed increase |
|---|---|---|
| 0° | 1.00 g | — |
| 30° | 1.15 g | ≈ 7 % |
| 45° | 1.41 g | ≈ 19 % |
| 60° | 2.00 g | ≈ 41 % |

Beyond 60° the load factor climbs very quickly: at 75° it is almost 4 g.

## Stall speed goes up

<Term id="stall">Stall</Term> speed increases with the **square root** of load factor:

**V<sub>S</sub> in a turn = V<sub>S</sub> · √n**

<MetarBreakdown parts={[
  ["VS = 50 kt", "Example stall speed in level flight"],
  ["60° bank", "n = 1 / cos 60° = 2 g"],
  ["√2 ≈ 1.41", "Stall speed is multiplied by 1.41"],
  ["VS ≈ 71 kt", "New stall speed in that turn"]

That is why a stall can happen at a speed that would seem safe in straight flight — for example, in a steep turn close to the ground.

## Structural limits

Each aircraft is certified for **limit load factors**, positive and negative, according to its category. **Manoeuvring speed** (V<sub>A</sub>) is, put simply, the speed below which the aircraft will stall before exceeding its limit load factor with an abrupt full control input; it decreases as the aircraft gets lighter.
