## Why a "standard" atmosphere is needed

The real atmosphere changes every day and everywhere. To calibrate instruments and compare aircraft performance, ICAO defines a reference model: the <Term id="isa">International Standard Atmosphere (ISA)</Term>.

| Quantity | ISA value at mean sea level |
|---|---|
| Temperature | 15 °C |
| Pressure | 1013.25 hPa (29.92 inHg) |
| Density | 1.225 kg/m³ |
| Temperature lapse rate | −6.5 °C/km (≈ −2 °C per 1,000 ft) up to 11 km |
| Tropopause | 11 km (≈ 36,090 ft), with a constant −56.5 °C up to 20 km |

Real temperatures are often described relative to ISA: if it is 15 °C at 5,000 ft, where ISA is 5 °C, the day is **ISA +10**.

## Pressure, temperature and density with altitude

- **Pressure:** always falls as you climb. Near sea level it drops roughly **1 inHg per 1,000 ft** (about 30 ft per hPa), and more slowly higher up.
- **Temperature:** in ISA it falls linearly up to the tropopause; the real atmosphere can have inversions.
- **Density:** falls as altitude increases, as **temperature rises** and, to a lesser extent, as **humidity rises** (water vapour is lighter than dry air).

## Pressure altitude and density altitude

- **Pressure altitude** is what the altimeter shows when set to 1013.25 hPa. It is the basis of <Term id="flight-level">flight levels</Term>; close to the ground, the <Term id="qnh" /> is used instead.
- <Term id="density-altitude">Density altitude</Term> is pressure altitude corrected for non-standard temperature: the ISA altitude at which the air would have the same density as the actual air.

No instrument shows density altitude, but the aircraft "feels" it. In less dense air:

- the wing needs **more true airspeed** to produce the same lift, so the aircraft takes off and lands faster over the ground;
- engines and propellers **produce less**;
- the take-off run is **longer** and the rate of climb **lower**.

<Callout type="caution" label="High, hot and humid">
A high airfield on a hot, humid day is the worst case for performance. Each aircraft's flight manual charts show how much longer the take-off becomes; this lesson explains why, it does not replace that data.
</Callout>
