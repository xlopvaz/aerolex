## Three axes through the centre of gravity

In flight an aeroplane can rotate about three imaginary axes that cross at its **centre of gravity**:

| Axis | Runs from… | Movement | Primary control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Longitudinal | Nose to tail | **Roll** (banking the wings) | <Term id="aileron">Ailerons</Term> |
| Lateral | Wingtip to wingtip | **Pitch** (nose up or down) | <Term id="elevator">Elevator</Term> |
| Vertical | Top to bottom, through the fuselage | **Yaw** (nose left or right) | <Term id="rudder">Rudder</Term> |

## The primary flight controls

- **Ailerons.** They sit on the trailing edge of the wings, near the tips, and move in **opposite** directions: when the pilot turns the yoke (or moves the stick) to the left, the left aileron goes up and the right one goes down. The wing with the lowered aileron produces more lift and rises; the aircraft banks to the left.
- **Elevator.** It is on the horizontal stabilizer. Pulling back on the controls raises the elevator, the tail goes down and the **nose goes up**. Pushing does the opposite.
- **Rudder.** It is on the fin and is moved with the **pedals**. Left pedal: the nose goes left.

<Callout type="info" label="How an aeroplane really turns">
An aeroplane does not turn with the rudder like a boat. To turn, the pilot **banks**: once the wings are tilted, part of the lift pulls the aircraft towards the inside of the turn. The rudder is used mainly to **coordinate** the turn and counter adverse yaw — the nose's tendency to swing the opposite way because the rising wing has more drag.
</Callout>

## The secondary flight controls

They adapt the wing to each phase of flight and reduce the pilot's workload:

- <Term id="flaps">Flaps</Term>: trailing-edge surfaces that, when extended, increase lift **and** drag. They let the aircraft fly more slowly with safety margin, which is why they are used for take-off and, above all, for landing.
- <Term id="slats">Slats</Term> and other leading-edge devices: they delay airflow separation so the wing can reach a higher angle of attack before it stalls.
- <Term id="spoilers">Spoilers</Term>: panels on the top of the wing that, when raised, **reduce lift and increase drag**. They help the aircraft descend or slow down in flight and, after touchdown, keep it firmly on the runway. On many aircraft they also assist with roll.
- <Term id="trim">Trim</Term>: tabs or surfaces adjusted so that the aircraft holds the desired attitude without the pilot having to hold pressure on the controls.
