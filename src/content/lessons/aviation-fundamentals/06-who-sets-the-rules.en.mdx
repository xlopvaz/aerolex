## Worldwide rules: ICAO

In November 1944, in the middle of the Second World War, delegates from 54 States met in Chicago to agree how civil aviation would work after the war. On **7 December 1944**, 52 of them signed the **Convention on International Civil Aviation**, known as the **Chicago Convention**.

The Convention gave rise to <Term id="icao" /> (the International Civil Aviation Organization), which worked on a provisional basis until **4 April 1947**, when enough States had ratified the Convention and it officially became ICAO. Its headquarters are in **Montréal** and today it has **193 Member States**.

ICAO does not fly aircraft or control traffic: it **adopts rules**. Its **Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs)** are published as **Annexes to the Convention**; you have already met some in this academy, such as Annex 3 (meteorology) and Annex 7 (aircraft and registration marks). Each State then writes them into its own law and must notify any differences.

## Europe: EASA

<Term id="easa" /> (the European Union Aviation Safety Agency) was created in **2002** by Regulation (EC) No 1592/2002 and is based in **Cologne**, Germany. It prepares the EU's common safety rules — which the Commission adopts as regulations, such as the Air Operations Regulation (965/2012) and the Aircrew Regulation (1178/2011) — certifies aircraft and other products, and checks that the rules are applied the same way in every Member State.

## United States: the FAA

The <Term id="faa" /> was born with the Federal Aviation Act of **1958** as the Federal Aviation Agency. On **1 April 1967** it became part of the new Department of Transportation and took its current name, Federal Aviation Administration. Unlike EASA, besides regulating it also **runs the country's air traffic control**.

## Spain: AESA

<Term id="aesa" /> (the State Aviation Safety Agency) was created by **Royal Decree 184/2008**. It is the national authority: it supervises, inspects and sanctions in matters of aviation safety, issues licences under the European rules and handles passenger complaints.

## What about IATA?

<Term id="iata" /> (the International Air Transport Association) **is not an authority**: it is the airlines' trade association. It publishes industry standards and assigns some very familiar codes, such as three-letter airport codes (MAD) and two-character airline codes (IB), which you will see in the next lesson.
