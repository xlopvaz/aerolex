## An aircraft is not the same as an aeroplane

An <Term id="aircraft" /> is any machine that is held up in the atmosphere by reactions of the air, **except** reactions of the air against the Earth's surface. That is why a hovercraft, which rides on a cushion of air pushed against the ground, is not an aircraft.

In everyday language people say "plane" for everything, but aviation is more precise: an **aeroplane** is just one kind of aircraft — the power-driven, fixed-wing kind.

## How ICAO classifies them

Annex 7 to the Chicago Convention sorts aircraft by **how they stay up** and whether they have an **engine**:

| | Not power-driven | Power-driven |
|---|---|---|
| **Lighter than air**: they float because they weigh less than the air they displace | Free balloon, captive balloon | Airship |
| **Heavier than air**: they are held up by aerodynamic forces | Glider, kite | Aeroplane, rotorcraft (helicopter and gyroplane), ornithopter |

What separates an aeroplane from a helicopter is the surface that produces lift: on an aeroplane the wings stay **fixed** in flight; on a helicopter, lift comes from **rotors** that spin.

## The five main parts of an aeroplane

Most aeroplanes share the same basic structure:

1. **<Term id="fuselage">Fuselage</Term>**: the main body. It carries the crew, passengers and cargo and joins all the other parts together.
2. **<Term id="wing">Wings</Term>**: they produce most of the lift. They carry the ailerons and flaps and, on many aircraft, the fuel.
3. **<Term id="empennage">Empennage</Term>**: the tail. It has a vertical stabilizer (fin) with the rudder and a horizontal stabilizer with the elevator.
4. **<Term id="landing-gear">Landing gear</Term>**: the wheels (or floats or skis) that support the aircraft on the ground. Most aircraft today have tricycle gear with a nosewheel; classic aircraft with a tailwheel have conventional gear.
5. **Powerplant**: the engine and propeller, or the jet engine, that produce thrust. It usually also provides electrical power and, on jets, pressurised air for other systems.

Look at any aircraft at an airport and try to point out the five parts. On airliners the engines are usually under the wings; on many regional and business jets, they are on the rear fuselage.
