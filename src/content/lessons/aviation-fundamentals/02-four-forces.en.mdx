## Four forces, two pairs

Four forces always act on an aeroplane in flight. They are easiest to understand in pairs:

| Force | Direction | What produces it |
|---|---|---|
| <Term id="lift">Lift</Term> | Upward, perpendicular to the relative wind | Air flowing past the wings |
| <Term id="weight">Weight</Term> | Downward, towards the centre of the Earth | Gravity acting on the aircraft, fuel, load and people |
| <Term id="thrust">Thrust</Term> | Forward | The engines (propeller or jet) |
| <Term id="drag">Drag</Term> | Rearward, in the direction of the relative wind | The air resisting the motion |

In **straight-and-level flight at constant speed** (no acceleration) the forces are in balance: lift equals weight and thrust equals drag. If either pair becomes unbalanced, the aircraft speeds up, slows down, climbs or descends until it finds a new balance.

## The wing: an airfoil

A wing's cross-section has a special shape, the <Term id="airfoil" />: a rounded **leading edge** at the front, a thin **trailing edge** at the back and usually more curvature on top than underneath. As it moves through the air, the airfoil deflects the airflow downward and creates a pressure difference: **lower pressure above, higher pressure below**.

The two usual explanations — Bernoulli (pressure difference) and Newton (the wing pushes air down, so the air pushes the wing up) — do not contradict each other: they describe the same phenomenon from two points of view.

Lift increases with **airspeed** (strongly: it depends on its square), **air density**, **wing area** and **angle of attack**.

## Angle of attack

The <Term id="angle-of-attack" /> is the angle between the airfoil's **chord line** (the straight line from leading edge to trailing edge) and the **relative wind** (the direction the air comes from relative to the wing).

<Callout type="caution" label="Not the same as nose-up">
Angle of attack is not the aircraft's attitude relative to the horizon. An aircraft can be nose-high with a small angle of attack (for example, in a steep climb) or nose-low with a large one (for example, when the pilot pulls back abruptly to recover from a dive).
</Callout>

## The stall

If the angle of attack keeps increasing, it reaches a point — the **critical angle of attack** — where the air can no longer follow the upper surface of the wing: it separates, lift drops sharply and drag rises steeply. That is a <Term id="stall" />.

What matters:

- A stall is caused by **exceeding the critical angle of attack**, not by flying slowly. It can happen at any airspeed, attitude or power setting.
- An aerodynamic stall has **nothing to do with the engine stopping**, even though English uses "stall" for both.
- The first step to recover is to **reduce the angle of attack**.

</Callout>
