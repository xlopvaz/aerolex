## Primary radar

**Primary radar** sends out pulses and receives the **echo** that bounces back off objects. It needs no cooperation from the aircraft: it sees anything that reflects the signal, including birds, rain or vehicles. But it does not know **who** each echo is or what **altitude** it is flying at.

## Secondary radar

**Secondary surveillance radar** (SSR) **interrogates** the aircraft and the on-board <Term id="transponder" /> **replies** with data:

| Mode | What it replies |
|---|---|
| **A** | A **4-digit** code from 0 to 7 (4,096 combinations) assigned by ATC |
| **C** | Also **pressure altitude** |
| **S** | Also a **unique 24-bit address** for the aircraft, allowing selective interrogation, and other data |

The code is assigned by ATC with the instruction <Term id="squawk" />, for example "squawk 4521".

## The special codes

Under ICAO procedures, three codes have a reserved meaning:

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **7500** | <Term id="unlawful-interference" /> |
| **7600** | Radio **communication** failure |
| **7700** | **Emergency** |

When they are selected, the controller gets an immediate alert on the screen.

## ADS-B

With **automatic dependent surveillance–broadcast** (<Term id="ads-b" />), the aircraft **broadcasts by itself** its position (normally calculated with GNSS), altitude, speed and identity, several times a second. Ground stations and other equipped aircraft receive it. It is very accurate but **depends on GNSS**: with jamming or spoofing, ADS-B information can be degraded or false.

## On board: TCAS and weather radar

- <Term id="tcas" /> uses the same principle as SSR: it interrogates nearby aircraft's transponders to detect conflicts (you saw this in Module 5).
- On-board <Term id="weather-radar" /> is a primary radar: it detects **precipitation** ahead of the aircraft, not clouds or clear-air turbulence.
