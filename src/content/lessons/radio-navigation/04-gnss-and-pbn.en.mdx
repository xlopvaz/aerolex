## How GNSS works

A <Term id="gnss" /> receiver measures how long the signals from several satellites take to arrive and works out its distance to each. According to the FAA AIM:

- the GPS constellation is based on **24 satellites**, arranged so that at least **5 are always visible** from any point;
- a three-dimensional position needs **4 satellites** (three for the position and one more to correct the receiver's clock).

Besides the US GPS there are other systems, such as Galileo (European), GLONASS (Russian) and BeiDou (Chinese).

## Integrity: RAIM and SBAS

A position alone is not enough for navigation: you need to know whether you can **trust** it.

- The receiver's <Term id="raim" /> uses extra satellites to check that they all agree. It needs at least **5 satellites** to **detect** a fault and **6** (or 5 with barometric aiding) to **exclude** the faulty satellite and keep navigating.
- <Term id="sbas" /> systems (EGNOS in Europe, WAAS in the USA) broadcast **corrections and integrity information** for a wide area from geostationary satellites. They allow approaches with vertical guidance such as **LPV**, with minima similar to an ILS CAT I.

## RNAV and RNP

<Term id="rnav">Area navigation</Term> lets an aircraft fly any path between points defined by coordinates, without having to fly from radio aid to radio aid. ICAO's performance-based navigation (<Term id="pbn" />) concept defines **what accuracy** that navigation must achieve in each phase:

- An **RNAV** specification requires an accuracy, for example **RNAV 5**: staying within **5 NM** of the track for at least **95 %** of the flight time.
- An <Term id="rnp" /> specification also requires the aircraft to **monitor its own performance and alert** the crew if it no longer meets it.

| Specification | Typical use |
|---|---|
| RNAV 10 / RNP 4 | Oceanic and remote routes |
| RNAV 5 | Continental routes |
| RNAV 1 / RNP 1 | Departures (SID) and arrivals (STAR) |
| RNP APCH | RNP approaches: LNAV, LNAV/VNAV, LPV |
| RNP AR APCH | Approaches needing special authorisation, in demanding environments |

## Jamming and spoofing

Since 2022, cases of **jamming** (the signal is blocked) and <Term id="gnss-spoofing">spoofing</Term> (false signals are broadcast) have risen sharply, especially near conflict zones. EASA has published and updated a safety information bulletin on the subject, whose fourth revision dates from July 2026. It warns that **spoofing is more dangerous** than jamming, because it is harder to detect and can mislead several systems at once.

Symptoms highlighted by EASA:

- **discrepancies** between the positions of different navigation systems;
- **abnormal** differences between groundspeed and TAS;
- **time jumps**;
- **false** TAWS alerts;
- deviations in the hybrid inertial/GNSS position.

The response is to **detect** the problem, **inform** ATC, rely on **inertial navigation and conventional radio aids** (VOR, DME, ILS) and know the operator's procedures.
