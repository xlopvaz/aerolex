## The declared distances

Each runway publishes four distances:

| Distance | What it is |
|---|---|
| <Term id="tora" /> | Take-off run available: the runway suitable for the ground run |
| <Term id="toda" /> | TORA + <Term id="clearway" /> |
| <Term id="asda" /> | TORA + <Term id="stopway" /> |
| <Term id="lda" /> | Landing distance available (may be shorter if the threshold is displaced) |

So a runway can be short for stopping but long enough for take-off if there is a clearway beyond it, or the other way round.

## The take-off speeds

- **VMCG**: the minimum speed at which, if an engine fails on the ground, directional control can be kept with the aerodynamic controls alone.
- <Term id="v1" />: the **decision speed**. If the failure is recognised before V1, the take-off is **rejected**; from V1 onwards it is **continued**. V1 may not be less than VMCG.
- <Term id="vr" />: the **rotation** speed. V1 may not be greater than VR.
- <Term id="v2" />: the **take-off safety speed**, reached at 35 ft with an engine failed. Under the certification rules, for most transport aircraft it is not less than **1.13 times the reference stall speed** nor **1.10 times the air minimum control speed**.

The order is always: **VMCG ≤ V1 ≤ VR ≤ V2**.

## The take-off distance

For a transport aircraft on a dry runway, the take-off distance is the **greater** of:

1. the distance to **35 ft** with an **engine failure** recognised at V1; and
2. **115 %** of the distance to 35 ft with **all engines** operating.

The **accelerate-stop distance** is what is needed to accelerate to V1 and brake to a stop. The take-off is possible if:

- take-off run ≤ TORA;
- take-off distance ≤ TODA;
- accelerate-stop distance ≤ ASDA.

When the engine-failure take-off distance and the accelerate-stop distance are equal, the field is said to be **balanced**.

## What limits the take-off mass

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Mass** | More mass, more distance and poorer climb |
| <Term id="density-altitude" /> | Heat and elevation reduce thrust and lift: more distance |
| **Wind** | A headwind shortens the distance; a tailwind lengthens it. In the EU only **50 %** of the reported headwind is counted and at least **150 %** of the tailwind |
| **Slope** | Uphill, more take-off distance |
| **Runway condition** | Water, snow or ice lengthen the acceleration and especially the braking |
| **Flaps** | More flap shortens the run but worsens the climb |

The **climb gradient** and **obstacles** (Lesson 2), **tyre speed** and **brake energy** also limit the mass. The maximum take-off mass is the lowest of them all.

When the actual mass is below the allowed one, the take-off can be made with **reduced thrust**, for example with an <Term id="assumed-temperature" />: the calculation pretends it is hotter and the engines wear less.

<Callout type="info" label="Density altitude in numbers">
A rule of thumb: each degree above ISA temperature adds about 120 ft of density altitude. At 2,000 ft with 30 °C (ISA +19), the aircraft performs as if at about 4,300 ft.
</Callout>
