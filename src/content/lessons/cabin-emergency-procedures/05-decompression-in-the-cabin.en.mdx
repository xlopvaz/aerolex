<Callout type="official-procedure" label="The operator's manual prevails">
This lesson summarises general principles. The exact commands, positions, checklists and division of tasks are set by each airline's operations manual and approved training.
</Callout>

## How it is recognised

A rapid <Term id="decompression" /> is hard to miss:

- a loud **noise** or a whistling;
- sudden **fog** in the cabin, as the air cools abruptly and moisture condenses;
- **cold** and a draught;
- loose objects moving and ears "popping";
- the **automatic drop of the oxygen masks**.

A **slow** decompression may go unnoticed until symptoms of <Term id="hypoxia" /> appear: euphoria, tiredness, headache, bluish lips and nails, impaired judgement. That is why any odd sign, such as a whistling door, is watched.

## The first seconds

Passenger masks must drop **automatically** before cabin altitude exceeds 15,000 ft. At that moment the rule is **the same for everyone**:

1. **Grab the nearest mask** and put it on at once, even if it belongs to a passenger seat.
2. **Sit down and hold on**: in a free seat, on the floor next to one or on the jump seat.
3. **Tell passengers**, shouting and gesturing, to put on their masks and fasten their seat belts, and that adults should put theirs on before helping children.

Nobody goes along the aisle to help anyone until the aircraft reaches a safe altitude: a crew member who loses consciousness helps no one.

<Callout type="caution" label="Your mask first">
Hypoxia can incapacitate very quickly at high altitude, and a rapid decompression shortens the time of useful consciousness even further. That is why the mask comes before any other task.
</Callout>

## While the aircraft descends

The flight deck starts an **emergency descent** towards an altitude where people can breathe without oxygen. It is normal for the descent to be rapid and steep. The cabin crew stay seated with their masks on until the commander announces that it is safe to move, according to the operator's procedure.

## Afterwards

With the aircraft at a safe altitude, the crew:

- checks on **colleagues** and **passengers**, starting with those who do not respond;
- uses **portable oxygen bottles** to move around if still needed and for anyone who needs them;
- gives **first-aid oxygen** to passengers who require it. On pressurised aircraft, EU rules require first-aid oxygen to be carried for a proportion of the passengers;
- looks for **damage** in the cabin (a window, a door) and reports it;
- reports injuries and the overall situation to the commander, and prepares the cabin for landing.

<Callout type="info" label="Dialogue · Diálogo">
**Cabin crew (announcement):** Put on your masks! Fasten your seat belts! Yourself first, then children!

**Captain (later, on the interphone):** We are at 10,000 feet; you can now move around the cabin.

**Purser:** Copied. I will report injuries and damage in five minutes.

*TCP (anuncio): ¡Pónganse las máscaras! ¡Abróchense los cinturones! ¡Primero ustedes, después los niños! — Comandante (más tarde, por el interfono): Estamos a 10.000 pies; ya pueden moverse por la cabina. — Jefa de cabina: Recibido. Le informo en cinco minutos de heridos y daños.*
</Callout>
