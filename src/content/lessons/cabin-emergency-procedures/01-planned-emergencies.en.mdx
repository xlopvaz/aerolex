## Planned and unplanned

- **Planned emergency**: there is time to brief the crew and passengers and prepare the cabin. For example, a system failure that calls for an emergency landing twenty minutes later.
- **Unplanned emergency**: there is no time to prepare anything. For example, a rejected take-off that ends off the runway.

In both, the cabin crew's job is the same: protect the passengers during the impact and get them out of the aircraft if needed. What changes is how much can be done in advance.

## The NITS briefing

When the commander decides to prepare the cabin, they call the <Term id="senior-cabin-crew-member">senior cabin crew member</Term> and give a briefing in a fixed format, **NITS**:

| Letter | Stands for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **N** | Nature of the problem | "We have a hydraulic failure" |
| **I** | Intention of the commander | "We will land in Valencia; possible evacuation" |
| **T** | Time available | "You have 15 minutes" |
| **S** | Special instructions | "The brace signal will be 'brace, brace'; do not use the left-hand exits" |

The person receiving the briefing **reads it back** to confirm they have understood it, and everyone **synchronises watches** with the time available. The senior cabin crew member then gathers the cabin crew and passes on the same information.

**Captain:** Purser, NITS briefing. Nature: right engine failure. Intention: return to Barcelona, normal landing, no evacuation expected. Time: 20 minutes, it is now 14:32. Special: cabin secured by 14:47.

**Purser:** Right engine failure, return to Barcelona, no evacuation expected, 20 minutes from 14:32, cabin secured by 14:47.

*Comandante: Jefa de cabina, información NITS. Naturaleza: fallo de motor derecho. Intención: regreso a Barcelona, aterrizaje normal, sin evacuación prevista. Tiempo: 20 minutos, ahora son las 14:32. Especial: cabina asegurada a las 14:47.*
</Callout>

## Preparing the cabin

The order and detail are set by the operator, but the typical tasks are:

1. **Secure the galley**: trolleys and containers locked, electrical equipment off.
2. **Brief the passengers** with the emergency announcement: what is going to happen, the brace position, the nearest exits and, for a ditching, life jackets.
3. **Choose and brief** <Term id="able-bodied-passenger">able-bodied passengers</Term> next to the exits.
4. **Remove** items that could injure people or slow the evacuation, stow baggage and check seat belts, seat backs and tables.
5. **Report** to the commander that the cabin is prepared and **sit** on the jump seat, mentally going over your own tasks (often called a *silent review*).

The <Term id="evacuation-slide">slides</Term> must be **armed** and passengers must know they will leave all their baggage behind.

## The brace position

The <Term id="brace-position">brace position</Term> reduces injuries in an impact in two ways: it limits **violent movement** of the body and places the head **against the surface** it would hit anyway. FAA guidance for forward-facing seats with lap belts, revised after impact tests, stresses:

- feet **on the floor and as far back as possible**, so the legs are not broken against the seat in front;
- leaning forward with the **head against the seat back** in front, or as low as possible;
- in rear-facing seats (such as crew seats), back and head **against the seat back**.

The order to brace comes from the flight deck with an agreed signal, and the cabin crew shout it repeatedly until the aircraft stops.
