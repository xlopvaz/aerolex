## Just like a METAR…

After the header comes the **base section**: the conditions expected at the start of the validity period. They are written with the codes you already know:

<MetarBreakdown parts={[
  ["24012KT", "Forecast wind 240° at 12 knots"],
  ["9999", "Forecast visibility 10 km or more"],
  ["SCT030", "Forecast scattered cloud at 3,000 ft"]
]} />

`G` (gusts), `VRB`, `CAVOK`, `NSC` and the weather codes (`-RA`, `TSRA`, `BR`, `FG`…) are also used.

## …with some differences

- **Cloud:** a TAF only indicates the **CB** (cumulonimbus) type; TCU is not forecast.
- **No RVR, no dew point and, usually, no pressure.**
- **No weather group if none is expected:** as in a METAR, the group is left out.
- **NSW** (*no significant weather*) only appears in change groups, to indicate that significant weather **ends**.

<MetarBreakdown parts={[
  ["21015KT", "Wind 210° at 15 knots"],
  ["6000", "Visibility 6,000 m"],
  ["-RA", "Light rain"],
  ["BKN012", "Broken layer at 1,200 ft: forecast ceiling 1,200 ft"]
]} caption="Base section of a rainy TAF." />

<Callout type="regional-diff" label="ICAO / US">
In US TAFs visibility is in statute miles and `P6SM` means **more than 6 miles**. CAVOK is not used, and non-convective low-level wind shear groups such as `WS020/24045KT` (at 2,000 ft, wind 240° at 45 knots) may appear.
</Callout>
