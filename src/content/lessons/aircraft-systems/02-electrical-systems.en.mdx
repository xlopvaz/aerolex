## A light aircraft's system

Most light aircraft have a **14 V or 28 V direct current** system. Its components are an **alternator** (or generator), a **battery**, the master and alternator switches, the **bus bar** that distributes current, **fuses** or **circuit breakers**, a **voltage regulator** and an **ammeter** or **loadmeter**.

- The alternator gives enough current even at low rpm, which many DC generators cannot.
- To charge the battery, the output must be slightly higher than the battery voltage: a 12 V battery is fed at about 14 V.
- A blown fuse is replaced; a tripped circuit breaker is reset by hand.

### Reading the ammeter

| Indication | What it means |
|---|---|
| Plus | The battery is charging |
| Minus | More is being used than replaced: the battery is discharging |
| Full-scale minus | Alternator or generator failure |
| Full-scale plus | Regulator failure |

The **loadmeter** starts at zero and shows the percentage of the generator's capacity being used.

## A transport aircraft's network

A jet needs far more power: galley ovens, electric hydraulic pumps, windshield heat, avionics. That is why its main network is **three-phase alternating current at 115 V and 400 Hz**. A higher-voltage AC network gives several times more power per kilogram than a 28 V DC one, and at 400 Hz transformers and motors are smaller and lighter than at 50 or 60 Hz.

### The sources

- **Engine generators**: the engine turns at a variable speed, but the frequency must be constant. A **constant-speed drive** (CSD) between engine and generator solves this. When both are in a single unit it is called an <Term id="idg" />.
- **<Term id="apu" /> generator**: on the ground and as a backup in flight.
- **Ground power** (GPU): external 115 V, 400 Hz power at the stand.
- **<Term id="ram-air-turbine">RAT</Term>**: on many aircraft it drives an emergency generator.
- **Batteries**: the last resort, to start the APU and keep the essentials alive.

### The conversions

- **Transformer rectifier units** (TRU) convert 115 V AC into **28 V DC** for the DC buses.
- The **static inverter** does the opposite: it converts battery DC into AC to power essential equipment if the generators are lost.

### Buses and priorities

Power is distributed through **buses**: generator buses, essential buses and ground service buses, among others. **Bus tie contactors** allow one generator to supply the other's buses if it fails. A control system decides automatically which source feeds each bus.

If generation is lost, the system **sheds** non-essential loads, such as the galley, so as not to overload the remaining sources. In the worst case only the **essential buses** remain, with the minimum instruments and radios to complete the flight.

<Callout type="info" label="A trend: the more-electric aircraft">
Some recent designs replace part of the bleed air and hydraulics with electrical systems and more powerful generators. The principles of sources, conversions and priorities remain the same.
</Callout>
