## Detecting a fire

Engines and the <Term id="apu" /> use **continuous loops**: a sensing tube running through the protected zone. Depending on the type, its electrical resistance changes with heat or it contains a gas that expands and trips a pressure switch. There are usually **two loops** (A and B): to avoid false alarms, the fire warning is normally given when both detect, and if one fails the system can work on the other.

Cargo holds and lavatories use **smoke detectors**.

## Fighting an engine fire

Each engine's **fire handle** is the heart of the procedure. Pulling it:

- closes the engine's **fuel shut-off valve**;
- closes that engine's **hydraulics** and **bleed air**;
- disconnects its **generator**;
- **arms the extinguisher bottles**.

Then, by turning it or pressing the relevant button, a bottle is **discharged** into the engine. Normally **two shots** are available per engine: if the warning persists after a set time, the second is fired.

Fixed extinguishers for engines and holds usually use **halon**, an agent that interrupts the fire's chemical reaction.

## Holds and lavatories

- Passenger aircraft **cargo holds** have smoke detection and, depending on their certification class, an extinguishing system fired from the flight deck. An initial discharge puts the fire out and a later, slower one keeps the agent concentration up for the time needed to reach an airport.
- Each **lavatory** must have a **smoke detector** and an **automatic extinguisher** in each waste bin, because cigarette ends thrown into them have caused fires.

<Callout type="caution" label="Smoke of unknown origin">
A hidden fire (behind panels, in the wiring) can become uncontrollable within minutes. Procedures insist on not wasting time: prepare to land as soon as possible while the source is sought.
</Callout>

## Crew oxygen

Flight crews use **quick-donning** masks that can be put on with one hand in a few seconds. The PHAK describes the regulators:

- **Diluter-demand**: supplies oxygen only on inhalation and mixes it with cabin air according to altitude. It is safe up to about **40,000 ft**.
- **Pressure-demand**: above about **34,000 ft** cabin altitude it supplies oxygen under **positive pressure**, which makes it safe to use above 40,000 ft.

In a high-altitude decompression the **100 %** setting is selected.

## Passenger oxygen

Passenger masks are **continuous-flow**, with a reservoir bag. On many aircraft the oxygen comes from **chemical generators** in the passenger service units above the seats:

- they are started by **pulling the mask** down;
- they produce oxygen through a chemical reaction with sodium chlorate, which generates **a lot of heat**;
- once started, they **cannot be switched off**, and they last a limited time.

That is why loose chemical oxygen generators are strictly regulated dangerous goods.

## When is oxygen required?

The US flight rules quoted in the PHAK for general aviation give a useful reference:

| Cabin pressure altitude | Requirement |
|---|---|
| 12,500 to 14,000 ft | Minimum crew, if more than **30 minutes** |
| Above 14,000 ft | Minimum crew, **at all times** |
| Above 15,000 ft | Oxygen must be **provided** to **every** occupant |

The PHAK also recommends using oxygen above **10,000 ft by day** and **5,000 ft at night**, because night vision is very sensitive to a lack of oxygen.
