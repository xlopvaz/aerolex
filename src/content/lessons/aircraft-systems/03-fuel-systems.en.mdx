## The tanks

On transport aircraft the fuel is carried mainly in the **wings**, in integral tanks: the sealed structure itself is the tank. There is also a **centre tank** in the fuselage between the wings, and some long-haul aircraft have a tank in the horizontal stabiliser that is also used to adjust the <Term id="centre-of-gravity">centre of gravity</Term> in cruise.

On many aircraft the **centre tank is used first**. Wing fuel relieves the bending caused by lift, so it is best kept for as long as possible.

## From tank to engine

1. Electric **boost pumps** inside the tanks deliver fuel under pressure to the engines and prevent cavitation and vapour forming in the lines.
2. A **shut-off valve** for each engine, also closed by the fire handle, isolates the engine from the fuel.
3. The **engine-driven pump** raises the pressure and the **fuel control** (today, the FADEC) meters the flow.
4. A **heat exchanger** usually warms the fuel with engine oil, which also cools the oil and prevents ice crystals forming in the filters.

If the boost pumps fail, many engines can keep drawing fuel by **suction**, with altitude limitations given in the manual.

## Crossfeed

The **crossfeed valve** connects the left and right systems, so that any tank can feed any engine. It is used mainly to **correct a fuel imbalance** between the wings, for example after flying with one engine shut down. Manuals set a **maximum imbalance**.

## Measuring and loading fuel

- Tank probes are **capacitance** probes and quantity is shown as **mass** (kg or lb), not volume. The fuel's energy depends on its mass, and its density changes with temperature.
- Refuelling is done under **pressure** from a single point under the wing, and the system closes each tank when it is full.
- Before refuelling the aircraft is bonded to dissipate **static electricity** and avoid a spark.

## Contamination

- **Water**: condenses in the tanks and can freeze at altitude. It is drained through the sumps.
- **Microorganisms**: bacteria and fungi living at the boundary between water and fuel. They form sludge that blocks filters and promotes tank corrosion.
- **Wrong fuel**: loading a fuel type other than the one intended is a serious hazard. Remember the colours from Module 3.

## Management in flight

At regular intervals the crew compares the fuel on board with the **flight-plan figure** and with the fuel **burned by the engines**. EU operating rules require these in-flight checks. A difference between what is on board and what should be can indicate a **leak**, and opening the crossfeed before ruling out a leak could drain the other side too.

Some aircraft, mostly long-haul, have a <Term id="fuel-jettison">fuel jettison</Term> system to get down to the maximum landing weight if they have to return shortly after take-off.
