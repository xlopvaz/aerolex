## Anti-ice and de-ice

- **Anti-ice**: **prevents** ice from forming. It is switched on before entering icing conditions.
- **De-ice**: **removes** ice that has already formed. It lets a layer build up and then breaks it.

<Term id="icing">Icing</Term> adds weight and drag, reduces lift and the stalling angle of attack, can block instrument probes and, in engines, damage the fan or cause a flameout if the ice breaks off inwards.

## What is protected and how

| Area | Typical system |
|---|---|
| Wing leading edges (jets) | Thermal anti-ice with hot bleed air |
| Engine intake lips | Thermal anti-ice with bleed air |
| Leading edges (turboprops and light aircraft) | **Pneumatic boots** that inflate to break the ice |
| Some light aircraft | "Weeping wing": antifreeze fluid through holes in the leading edge |
| Propellers | Alcohol or electric heating elements on the blades |
| Windshields | Electric heating elements in the glass |
| Pitot tubes, static ports, angle-of-attack and temperature probes | Electric heating |

Windshield heat has a second purpose: warm glass is more flexible and resists a bird strike better. That is why it is usually on for the whole flight, not just in icing.

**Pneumatic boots** are inflated with pressurised air and deflated in cycles. The PHAK says the pilot can operate them as soon as ice accumulates.

## Detecting ice

The crew looks for ice on visible reference points, such as the windshield wipers. Many aircraft also have **ice detectors**: for example, a probe vibrating at a set frequency, which falls as ice builds up on it. The detector gives a warning or switches the systems on automatically.

## Rain

To keep a clear view in heavy rain, aircraft use **wipers**, **rain repellent** and **hydrophobic coatings** on the glass. On fast aircraft, wipers have a maximum operating speed.

## Ground de-icing

No on-board system protects a parked aircraft. The **clean-aircraft concept** applies: no take-off with frost, ice or snow adhering to critical surfaces (wings, stabilisers, control surfaces). Even a thin layer as rough as sandpaper can reduce lift considerably.

The process has one or two steps:

1. **<Term id="de-icing">De-icing</Term>**: contamination is removed with hot fluid (usually **Type I**).
2. **Anti-icing**: a thickened fluid (**Type II** or **Type IV**) is applied to form a protective film.

Each fluid has a **holdover time**, which depends on the fluid type, the temperature and the type and intensity of precipitation. If it runs out before take-off, or the fluid is seen to have failed, the treatment must be repeated.

<Callout type="official-procedure" label="De-icing programme">
Holdover times come from tables published by the authorities and adopted by each operator in its de-icing programme. The decision to take off in icing is never based on a guess.
</Callout>
