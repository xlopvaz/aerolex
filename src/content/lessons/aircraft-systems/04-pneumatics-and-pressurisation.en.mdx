## Bleed air

<Term id="bleed-air">Bleed air</Term> is taken from the engines' compressor stages (or from the <Term id="apu" />). It comes out very hot and under pressure, so it goes through a **precooler** before being distributed. It is used for:

- **air conditioning** and **pressurisation**;
- wing and engine **anti-ice**;
- engine **starting**;
- pressurising the hydraulic reservoirs and, on some aircraft, driving hydraulic pumps.

## Air conditioning packs

Each **pack** cools bleed air through heat exchangers cooled by outside ram air and an **air cycle machine**: the air is compressed, cooled, and then expanded through a turbine, which cools it much further. It is then dried and mixed with hot air and with **recirculated** cabin air to reach the temperature wanted in each zone.

## Pressurisation

At 35,000 ft the outside pressure is roughly a quarter of the sea-level value. To keep a comfortable <Term id="cabin-altitude">cabin altitude</Term>, the aircraft becomes a pressure vessel.

The system does not control how much air comes **in** (the packs provide that), but how much goes **out**: the <Term id="outflow-valve">outflow valve</Term> opens or closes to hold the scheduled cabin altitude.

The US certification standard requires that, under normal conditions, cabin altitude does not exceed **8,000 ft** at the aircraft's maximum operating altitude. It also requires a **warning** to the crew if cabin altitude exceeds **10,000 ft**.

### Differential pressure

**Differential pressure** is the difference between cabin and outside pressure, and the structure has a maximum. An example from the FAA PHAK:

- at 28,000 ft the standard pressure is **4.8 psi**;
- with a differential of **6.1 psi**, the cabin is at 4.8 + 6.1 = **10.9 psi**;
- that corresponds to a cabin altitude of about **8,000 ft**.

That is why the higher an aircraft flies, the greater the differential it needs to keep the same cabin.

### Safety valves

- The **pressure relief valve** opens if the differential exceeds the maximum.
- The **vacuum (negative) relief valve** stops outside pressure exceeding inside pressure, for example in a very fast descent.
- The **dump valve** depressurises the cabin at will, for example before opening the doors in an evacuation.

## Decompression

| Type | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Explosive** | The cabin loses pressure faster than the lungs can release air (under half a second) |
| **Rapid** | The cabin loses pressure in seconds, but the lungs can keep up |
| **Slow** | A small leak makes the cabin climb gradually. It is the most insidious, because hypoxia can set in unnoticed |

A rapid decompression can cut the **time of useful consciousness** to between a third and a quarter of normal. The crew's actions always start the same way: **put on the <Term id="oxygen-mask">oxygen mask</Term>** and establish communication, then, if needed, start an **emergency descent** to a safe altitude.

On transport aircraft flying above 25,000 ft, the passenger masks must **drop automatically** before cabin altitude exceeds **15,000 ft**.

<Callout type="caution" label="Hypoxia gives no warning">
The symptoms of hypoxia (euphoria, impaired judgement, reduced vision) can go unnoticed by the person suffering them. That is why cabin altitude warnings and mask discipline matter so much.
</Callout>
