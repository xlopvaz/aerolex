## Latitude and longitude

Any point on Earth is located by two angles:

- **Latitude**: the angular distance north or south of the **equator**, from 0° to 90° N or S. Lines of equal latitude are **parallels**.
- **Longitude**: the angular distance east or west of the **Greenwich meridian**, from 0° to 180° E or W. Lines of equal longitude are **meridians**.

Each degree is divided into 60 minutes. For example, Madrid-Barajas airport is at roughly 40° 28′ N, 3° 34′ W.

## The nautical mile

The **nautical mile** (NM) is **1,852 m** and corresponds approximately to **one minute of arc of latitude**. So:

- **1° of latitude ≈ 60 NM** anywhere in the world;
- whereas a degree of **longitude** is 60 NM only at the equator and gets shorter towards the poles (half that at 60° latitude).

This relationship makes measuring distances on a chart very easy: just use the latitude scale along the edge.

## Great circle and rhumb line

- A <Term id="great-circle" /> is where the Earth meets a plane through its centre. The great-circle arc between two points is the **shortest path** between them, but its direction **keeps changing** (except along the equator or a meridian).
- A <Term id="rhumb-line" /> crosses every meridian at the **same angle**: it is flown on a constant track, but it is **longer**.

Over short distances the difference is negligible. On ocean crossings it is large, which is why long-haul routes follow great circles that, on an ordinary map, look like curves bending towards the pole.

## Projections

No flat chart shows the Earth without distortion. Aviation mainly uses two **conformal** projections, which preserve angles:

| Projection | Main feature | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Mercator** | A rhumb line is a **straight line**; scale grows sharply towards the poles | Marine navigation charts, some equatorial charts |
| **Lambert conformal conic** | A great circle is **almost a straight line** and scale is nearly constant across the area shown | Most aeronautical charts, such as FAA sectionals |

## Scale

**Scale** relates a distance on the chart to the real one. FAA sectional charts, for example, are **1:500,000**: 1 cm on the chart is 5 km on the ground. The larger the denominator, the more area the chart covers and the less detail it shows.

## Time

The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, that is, **15° per hour**. Aviation always uses **UTC** (coordinated universal time), the same everywhere, for flight plans, weather reports and communications.
