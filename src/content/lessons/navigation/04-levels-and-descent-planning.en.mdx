## Semicircular levels

To separate traffic flying in opposite directions, the rules of the air assign <Term id="flight-level">cruising levels</Term> by **magnetic track**:

| Magnetic track | IFR | VFR |
|---|---|---|
| **000° to 179°** | **Odd** thousands: FL 70, 90, 110… | Odd **+ 500 ft**: FL 75, 95, 115… |
| **180° to 359°** | **Even** thousands: FL 80, 100, 120… | Even **+ 500 ft**: FL 85, 105, 125… |

So an eastbound and a westbound IFR flight are separated vertically, and VFR flights sit halfway between IFR levels. Some States apply other tables in certain areas, published in their AIP.

## RVSM

Between **FL290 and FL410**, in **reduced vertical separation minimum** airspace (<Term id="rvsm" />), the separation between levels is **1,000 ft** instead of 2,000. The table still applies: FL 290, 310, 330… eastbound and FL 300, 320, 340… westbound. To fly in RVSM, the aircraft and operator need a specific approval, with more accurate altimeters and autopilot.

## When to start down?

A **3°** descent path loses about **318 ft per nautical mile**. The usual rule of thumb rounds the sum:

> descent distance (NM) ≈ height to lose (in thousands of feet) × 3

which is equivalent to a path slightly steeper than 3°, leaving a small margin.

Example: from **FL350** to **3,000 ft**, you need to lose about 32,000 ft → 32 × 3 ≈ **96 NM** before the point where you want to be at 3,000 ft. In practice a margin is added to slow down and for any tailwind.

## At what rate?

To hold a 3° path:

> rate of descent (ft/min) ≈ groundspeed (kt) × 5

Example: on an ILS approach at **140 kt** groundspeed → about **700 ft/min**. With a headwind, groundspeed is lower and less vertical speed is needed; with a tailwind, more.

<Callout type="tip" label="Why they work">
3° is a gradient of just over 5 %: about 318 ft per nautical mile. At 60 kt you cover 1 NM a minute, so at any groundspeed the rate of descent is about 5 times that speed.
</Callout>
