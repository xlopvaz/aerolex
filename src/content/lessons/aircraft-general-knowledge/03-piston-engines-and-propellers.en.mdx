## The four-stroke cycle

Most light aircraft use **four-stroke** piston engines: **intake** (the fuel-air mixture comes in), **compression**, **power** (the spark plug ignites it and the gases push the piston down) and **exhaust**.

Ignition comes from two independent **magnetos**, each with its own set of spark plugs. Magnetos make their own current as the engine turns, so the engine keeps running even if the aircraft's electrical system fails: the master switch powers every circuit **except the ignition**. Two systems give redundancy and more complete combustion.

## Mixture

As the aircraft climbs, the air gets thinner and the same amount of fuel makes the mixture too **rich**: the engine loses power, runs rough and fouls its plugs. That is why the pilot **leans** the mixture with altitude. A mixture that is too **lean** at high power overheats the cylinders and encourages detonation.

## Carburettor ice

In the carburettor, fuel vaporisation and the pressure drop in the venturi cool the air. If there is moisture, ice forms and chokes the flow of mixture. According to the FAA PHAK:

- it is most likely with temperatures **below 21 °C (70 °F)** and relative humidity **above 80 %**;
- it can happen even at **38 °C (100 °F)** with **50 %** humidity.

The symptom with a fixed-pitch propeller is a **drop in rpm**. Carburettor heat prevents or removes it. When applied with ice already present, the rpm drops further for a moment (warm, less dense air and water from the melting ice) and then rises.

## Detonation and pre-ignition

- **Detonation**: the mixture explodes suddenly and uncontrollably in the cylinder instead of burning progressively. It goes with high cylinder-head temperatures and high power. Typical causes are a lower fuel grade than specified, very high manifold pressure with low rpm, an over-lean mixture at high power and long climbs with little cooling.
- **Pre-ignition**: the mixture ignites too early from a hot spot, such as a carbon deposit.

The two can occur together and one can cause the other.

## Fuels

Piston aviation fuel (AVGAS) is identified by colour: **80 red**, **100 green** and **100LL blue**. Jet fuel (Jet A) is **colourless or straw-coloured**. Putting Jet A into a piston engine causes severe detonation and engine failure, hence the importance of checking the fuel before every flight. Water in the fuel, found by sampling the drains, can freeze and block lines or stop the engine.

## Turbocharging

A **turbocharger** uses the exhaust gases to drive a turbine that compresses the intake air. This lets the engine keep its manifold pressure, and therefore its power, up to much higher altitudes. A **wastegate** controls how much exhaust gas goes through the turbine.

## Propellers

- **Fixed pitch**: the blade angle is a compromise between climb and cruise.
- **Constant speed**: a **governor** changes the pitch automatically to hold the rpm the pilot selects. The pilot has two controls: the **throttle** (power, read on the **manifold pressure** gauge) and the **propeller lever** (rpm). Outside the range between the pitch stops, the rpm varies with airspeed again.
- **Feathering**: on twins, if an engine fails, the blades are turned edge-on to the airflow. The propeller stops being driven round by the air (*windmilling*) and drag falls dramatically.

## FADEC

**FADEC** is a digital computer that controls the engine (and propeller) with a single lever. It does away with magnetos, carburettor heat, the mixture lever and priming, and adjusts fuel and ignition for each cylinder. It has **two identical channels** and needs a backup electrical source, because a total failure could mean losing thrust.

<Callout type="caution" label="Ice on a fine day">
Carburettor ice needs neither cloud nor cold: a humid spring day, with reduced power on the descent, is a classic case.
</Callout>
