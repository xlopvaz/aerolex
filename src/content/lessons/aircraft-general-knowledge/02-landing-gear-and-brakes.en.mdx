## Configuration

<Term id="landing-gear">Landing gear</Term> can be **fixed** or **retractable**, and either **tricycle** (nosewheel) or **conventional** (tailwheel). Transport aircraft use retractable tricycle gear: a <Term id="nose-gear">nose gear</Term> and two or more main legs, which on large aircraft carry four- or six-wheel *bogies* to spread the weight over the pavement.

## Shock struts

Each leg has an **oleo-pneumatic shock strut**: a cylinder holding hydraulic fluid and nitrogen (or air). On touchdown the fluid is forced through a metering orifice and absorbs the energy of the impact, while the gas acts as a spring and supports the aircraft's weight on the ground.

## Steering

Light aircraft steer on the ground with the rudder pedals, which move the nosewheel, and with **differential braking**. Airliners have **hydraulic nosewheel steering**, commanded by a side **tiller** for tight turns and by the pedals for small corrections during the take-off and landing roll.

## Brakes

Transport aircraft use hydraulically operated **multiple-disc** brakes on the main wheels. Many have **carbon** discs: they are lighter than steel and cope better with high temperatures. A high-speed rejected take-off can heat them so much that, minutes later, they become a wheel-fire risk.

### Anti-skid

The <Term id="anti-skid">anti-skid system</Term> compares each wheel's speed with the aircraft's speed and, if a wheel starts to slow too much, releases its pressure for an instant. It has four functions:

- **Normal skid control**: releases pressure when a wheel starts to skid.
- **Locked-wheel protection**: fully releases the brake of a wheel that locks.
- **Touchdown protection**: prevents braking before the wheels have spun up.
- **Fail-safe protection**: if the system fails, it reverts to manual braking and warns the crew.

### Autobrake

The <Term id="autobrake">autobrake</Term> applies the brakes after touchdown to achieve the selected **deceleration** (usually there are several levels). For take-off it is armed in **RTO** (*rejected take-off*) mode: if the take-off is rejected above a certain speed, it brakes with maximum pressure. The pilot disconnects it by pressing the brake pedals or advancing the thrust levers.

## Tyres

Aircraft tyres run at much higher pressures than car tyres. On large transport aircraft, braked wheels must be inflated with **dry nitrogen** or another inert gas. Nitrogen does not feed a fire and does not carry moisture that would freeze at altitude. Wheels have **fusible plugs** that melt and deflate the tyre in a controlled way if the brakes overheat, so that the tyre does not burst.

## Retraction, indication and emergency

The gear is usually raised and lowered hydraulically. To prevent a retraction on the ground, a **squat switch** (*weight-on-wheels*) senses that the aircraft is resting on its gear and locks the lever. Three green lights show three legs **down and locked**. Many light aircraft also have a warning horn if power is reduced with the gear up.

If the normal system fails, there is an **emergency extension**, which depending on the aircraft may be:

- by **gravity** (free fall), releasing the up-locks;
- with a **hand pump**;
- with **compressed gas**.

<Callout type="official-procedure" label="The manual rules">
Autobrake levels, brake temperature limits and the emergency extension procedure are specific to each aircraft type. Always refer to the flight manual and the operator's procedures.
</Callout>
