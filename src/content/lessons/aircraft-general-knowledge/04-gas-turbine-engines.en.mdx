## Suck, squeeze, bang, blow

A gas turbine does the same job as a piston engine, but **continuously** and in separate sections instead of in a single cylinder:

1. **Intake**: brings air to the compressor with as little loss as possible.
2. **Compressor**: raises the air pressure a great deal. It may be **centrifugal**, **axial** or a combination of both.
3. **Combustion chamber**: fuel is injected and burned continuously at almost constant pressure.
4. **Turbine**: the hot gases spin it. Through a shaft, the turbine drives the compressor (and the fan or propeller, if there is one).
5. **Exhaust**: the gases leave through the nozzle and produce thrust.

Thrust is an application of Newton's third law: the engine accelerates a large mass of air backwards and receives an equal and opposite force forwards.

Airliner engines have **two or three concentric spools** turning at different speeds. In a two-spool engine, the **low-pressure spool** connects the fan and low-pressure compressor to the low-pressure turbine, and the **high-pressure spool** connects the high-pressure compressor to the high-pressure turbine.

## Engine types

| Type | How it produces thrust or power | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Turbojet** | All the air goes through the core and leaves at high speed | Older and military aircraft |
| **<Term id="turbofan">Turbofan</Term>** | A large fan accelerates air that goes around the core | Almost all transport jets |
| **Turboprop** | The turbine drives a propeller through a gearbox | Regional aircraft such as the ATR 72 |
| **Turboshaft** | The turbine delivers shaft power, not thrust | Helicopters and APUs |

The <Term id="bypass-ratio">bypass ratio</Term> is the mass of air going through the fan around the core divided by the mass going through the core. A high ratio means accelerating **a lot of air a little** rather than **a little air a lot**, which is more efficient and quieter. In high-bypass turbofans, most of the thrust comes from the fan.

## Engine parameters

- **N1**: low-pressure spool (fan) speed, as a **percentage** of design rpm. On many engines it is the parameter used to set thrust.
- **N2**: high-pressure spool speed, also as a percentage.
- <Term id="egt" />: the temperature of the gases in the turbine. It is a **limiting parameter**. Depending on where it is measured it has other names: TIT, TOT, ITT or TGT.
- **EPR** (*engine pressure ratio*): turbine discharge pressure divided by compressor inlet pressure. Some manufacturers set thrust with EPR instead of N1.
- **Torque**: measures shaft power on turboprops and turboshafts.

## When things go wrong

### Compressor stall

Compressor blades are small aerofoils. If their angle of attack exceeds the critical angle, the flow separates just as it does on a wing: this is a <Term id="compressor-stall">compressor stall</Term>. A transient stall gives a single **bang**. A steady stall gives strong **vibration** and a **roar**, with fluctuating rpm and **rising EGT**. Recovery is to reduce power quickly, decrease the aircraft's angle of attack and increase airspeed.

### Flameout

The flame goes out in the combustion chamber:

- **rich flameout**: too much fuel, often from a very fast acceleration;
- **lean flameout**: at high altitude with low fuel pressure and low engine speed, or in a descent with the engine throttled back.

### Abnormal starts

- **Hot start**: EGT exceeds the limit, from too much fuel or too little turbine rpm. The manual states which inspection is required.
- **Hung start**: the engine lights but **does not accelerate to idle**, from insufficient starter power or a fuel control fault.

<Callout type="info" label="Why EGT matters">
The temperature the turbine blades can take is a jet engine's great limit. That is why EGT is watched closely during start and take-off, and why engines lose EGT margin as they age.
</Callout>
