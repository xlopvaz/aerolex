## Thrust ratings

A certificated jet engine has several ratings. The two fundamental ones are:

- **Take-off thrust**: the maximum approved, limited in time. The FAA definition restricts it to periods of **not more than 5 minutes** for take-off operation.
- **Maximum continuous thrust**: thrust that can be used without a time limit. It is used, for example, to hold altitude after losing an engine.

## Hot air and altitude steal thrust

Thrust depends on the **mass of air** the engine can accelerate. With **high temperature** or **high altitude** the air is less dense and the available thrust falls. That is why summer take-offs from high airports are the most demanding and can limit the take-off weight.

## Reduced and derated thrust

When the runway and weight allow it, take-offs are almost never made with full thrust: using less extends engine life and improves reliability. FAA Advisory Circular AC 25-13 distinguishes two methods:

- **Reduced thrust (assumed temperature)**: the engine is given an outside air temperature **higher than the real one**. It delivers the thrust it would give in that heat, less than the maximum. Minimum control speeds are still based on maximum thrust, so **the pilot can apply full thrust at any time**. Airbus calls it **FLEX**.
- **Derate**: a **lower** rating is used, certificated as such, with its own speeds and limitations calculated for that lower thrust. Because the control speeds depend on it, full thrust cannot simply be applied if an engine fails at low speed. Its advantage is that it allows heavier take-offs from short, wet or contaminated runways.

<Callout type="caution" label="They are not the same">
With an assumed temperature there is always margin to push the levers to maximum. With a derate, pushing them at low speed after an engine failure can leave the aircraft below its minimum control speed.
</Callout>

## What you notice moving from propeller to jet

The FAA *Airplane Flying Handbook* sums up the differences that surprise pilots most:

- **Slow response from idle**: a jet takes several seconds to spool up from low rpm. That is why, on the approach, the engines are kept at some thrust rather than being allowed to reach idle too early.
- **No propeller slipstream**: the wing does not get the accelerated air from a propeller, so at low speed there is no "extra" lift when power is added.
- **No propeller drag**: when you close the throttles, a propeller slows the aircraft; a jet does not. A clean jet decelerates slowly and struggles to descend and slow down at the same time. Hence **spoilers** or speed brakes.
- **Narrow speed margins at altitude**: high up, the minimum speed and Mach buffet come together (you saw this in Module 2).

## Thrust reversers

<Term id="thrust-reverser">Thrust reversers</Term> redirect part of the flow forwards after touchdown. They are **most effective at high speed**, so they are used right after touchdown and brought back to reverse idle as the aircraft slows. At low speed they can make the engine re-ingest its own hot exhaust or debris blown up from the ground, risking a compressor stall or foreign-object damage.

## Continuous ignition and fuel heaters

- **Continuous ignition** keeps the igniters firing so that the engine relights at once if the flame goes out. It is used for take-off and landing and in heavy rain, icing or turbulence, as the manual specifies.
- **Fuel heaters** stop water dissolved in the fuel from forming ice crystals that would block the filters.
- Fuel has a limit of its own too: **Jet A-1** has a maximum freezing point of **−47 °C** and **Jet A** of **−40 °C**. On long flights through very cold areas the fuel temperature is monitored.
