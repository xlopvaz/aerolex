## Hypoxia

With altitude the air pressure drops and, with it, the **partial pressure of oxygen**: the air still has 21 % oxygen, but the lungs absorb it less well. The result is <Term id="hypoxia" />.

The danger is that it **does not hurt**: it brings euphoria, false confidence, tunnel vision, slow thinking, tingling and bluish lips or nails. The hypoxic person is usually the last to notice.

### Time of useful consciousness

This is how long a person can keep acting effectively without enough oxygen. The guide values published by the FAA:

| Altitude | Time of useful consciousness |
|---|---|
| 18,000 ft | 20–30 minutes |
| 25,000 ft | 3–5 minutes |
| 30,000 ft | 1–2 minutes |
| 35,000 ft | 30–60 seconds |
| 40,000 ft | 15–20 seconds |

After a rapid <Term id="decompression" /> the time can be about **half**. That is why the first action is always the **oxygen mask**: your own first.

## Hyperventilation

<Term id="hyperventilation" /> is breathing more than needed, often from stress. It lowers the carbon dioxide in the blood and brings **dizziness, tingling and breathlessness**, very similar to hypoxia. If in doubt and at altitude, treat it first as **hypoxia** (oxygen). If not, **control the breathing**: slower, talking out loud.

## Trapped gases and decompression sickness

- On the way up, gas in the **middle ear** and **sinuses** escapes easily; on the way **down** it has to get back in. With a cold, the Eustachian tube can block and cause severe pain or even injury. Flying with a cold is a bad idea.
- Gas in the **gut** and in **teeth** with decay or faulty fillings also expands.
- When diving, the body absorbs **nitrogen**. Going up in altitude afterwards can form bubbles: <Term id="decompression-sickness" />. The FAA recommends waiting at least **12 hours** after a dive with no decompression stops before flying up to 8,000 ft, and **24 hours** after dives with stops or before flying above 8,000 ft.

## Fitness to fly

Before each flight, the pilot runs a self-check. The FAA suggests <Term id="imsafe" />:

| Letter | Question |
|---|---|
| **I** (illness) | Am I ill? |
| **M** (medication) | Am I taking any medication that affects flying? |
| **S** (stress) | Am I under pressure or worried? |
| **A** (alcohol) | Have I been drinking recently? |
| **F** (fatigue) | Am I well rested? |
| **E** (emotion) | Am I emotionally fit? |

On **alcohol**, the FAA rule forbids acting as a crew member within **8 hours** of drinking, while under its influence or with a blood concentration of **0.04 %** or more. In Europe there are limits and testing too, and many operators are stricter. Alcohol also lowers tolerance to hypoxia.

**Medication**, even over the counter (drowsy antihistamines, for example), can incapacitate. The European medical rules (Part-MED) require pilots not to exercise the privileges of their licence if they know or suspect a decrease in medical fitness.

<Callout type="caution" label="Not flying is a valid decision">
Illness, medication or fatigue are not offset by experience. If the answer to any IMSAFE question is doubtful, you don't fly.
</Callout>
