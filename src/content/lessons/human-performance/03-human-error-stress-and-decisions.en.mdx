## Why we make mistakes

Human error is **normal**: expert pilots make errors too. Safety is not about eliminating it but about **detecting and containing** it before it has consequences.

### The SHELL model

The <Term id="shell-model" /> model, used by ICAO in its human-factors training, puts the **person** at the centre and studies their interfaces:

| Interface | Example of a problem |
|---|---|
| Person – **Software** (procedures, checklists, charts) | A confusing checklist |
| Person – **Hardware** (equipment) | Two identical switches side by side |
| Person – **Environment** | Noise, heat, turbulence, night |
| Person – **Person** | Poor communication on the flight deck |

### The Swiss cheese model

In James Reason's <Term id="swiss-cheese-model" />, each **defence** (design, training, procedures, supervision, crew) is a slice with holes. An accident happens when the holes **line up**. Many holes are **latent conditions**, present in the organisation long before the accident; others are **active failures** by those at the front line. That is why investigations look for causes across the whole system, not for someone to blame.

## Stress, workload and attention

- A little stress **improves** performance; too much **wrecks** it: tunnel vision, forgetting, fixation on a single idea.
- Very high **workload** overloads; very low (a long, quiet cruise) dulls. Both extremes are dangerous.
- Attention is **limited**: focusing on a problem can mean losing control of the aircraft.
- <Term id="situational-awareness" /> has three levels: **perceiving** what is happening, **understanding** what it means and **anticipating** what will happen.

## Fatigue

<Term id="fatigue" /> reduces attention, memory and judgement, and those affected underestimate it. Its causes are lack of sleep, long hours awake, workload and disruption of the <Term id="circadian-rhythm" />: alertness is lowest in the early hours, roughly between 2 and 6 body time.

The EU flight time limitation rules set maximum duty and minimum rest, and require operators to manage fatigue risk. But the responsibility is also personal: resting when due and reporting when not fit.

## Hazardous attitudes

The FAA describes five attitudes that lead to poor decisions, each with its antidote:

| Attitude | Idea | Antidote |
|---|---|---|
| **Anti-authority** | "Don't tell me what to do" | Rules are usually right |
| **Impulsivity** | "Do something now" | Think before acting |
| **Invulnerability** | "It won't happen to me" | It could happen to me |
| **Macho** | "I can handle this" | Taking chances is foolish |
| **Resignation** | "What's the use?" | I can make a difference |

## Deciding in flight

Faced with a problem with no clear procedure, a method prevents rushing:

- **FORDEC**, widely used in Europe: **F**acts, **O**ptions, **R**isks and benefits, **D**ecision, **E**xecution and **C**heck.
- **DECIDE**, proposed by the FAA: **D**etect, **E**stimate, **C**hoose, **I**dentify, **D**o, **E**valuate.

What matters in both is the same: **fly the aircraft first**, understand the problem, consider more than one option and **review** the decision when things change.

<Callout type="info" label="Pressure to get there">
One of the best-known traps is pressure to complete the flight as planned even though conditions have changed. Setting personal limits and alternatives in advance helps avoid it.
</Callout>
