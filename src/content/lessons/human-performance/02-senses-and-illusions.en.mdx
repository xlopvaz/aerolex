## Why the senses deceive us

On the ground, orientation combines **vision**, the **inner ear** and body sensations. In flight, with no visible horizon, the inner ear takes over… and gets it wrong:

- The **semicircular canals** detect **rotation**, but stop sensing it after about 20 seconds of constant turning.
- The **otoliths** detect **linear acceleration** and gravity, but cannot tell tilting from accelerating.

The result is <Term id="spatial-disorientation" />. The defence is to **believe the instruments**, above all the attitude indicator.

## Vestibular illusions

| Illusion | What happens | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **The leans** | After an unnoticed slow roll, levelling the wings feels like a bank the other way | The pilot banks again |
| **Coriolis** | Moving the head during a prolonged turn causes a feeling of rotation in another axis | Very strong disorientation |
| **Graveyard spiral** | In a sustained turn the pilot stops feeling it; losing height, they pull back | The spiral tightens |
| <Term id="somatogravic-illusion" /> | Strong acceleration feels like **pitching up** | The pilot **lowers the nose** near the ground |
| **Inversion** | An abrupt change from climb to level feels like tumbling backwards | The pilot pitches down |
| **Elevator** | An updraft feels like climbing | The pilot pitches down |

The **somatogravic** illusion is especially dangerous in a **go-around** or a night take-off over the sea: the aircraft accelerates, the pilot feels it is climbing too steeply and pushes forward.

## Visual illusions on approach

| Situation | The pilot thinks they are… | Tends to fly… |
|---|---|---|
| **Upsloping** runway | Too high | **Low** |
| **Downsloping** runway | Too low | High |
| **Narrow** runway | Too high | **Low** |
| **Wide** runway | Too low | High |
| Featureless terrain, water, night (<Term id="black-hole-approach" />) | Too high | **Low** |
| Rain on the windscreen | Higher | Low |
| Fog or haze | Farther away | — (tends to pitch up on entering fog) |

The most dangerous are those that make you fly **low**. The defence: an electronic **glide path** or the <Term id="papi" />, checking height against distance (about 300 ft per nautical mile on a 3° path) and knowing the runway slope and width in advance.

<Callout type="tip" label="Before a night approach">
Review on the chart the slope, width and surroundings of the runway, and plan to follow an electronic or visual path all the way. If there is none, keep circuit height until the runway is at the right angle.
</Callout>
