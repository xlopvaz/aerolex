## From highs to lows

Air tends to move from areas of **high pressure** to areas of **low pressure**, pushed by the pressure difference. The closer together the **isobars** on a chart (lines of equal pressure), the greater that difference and **the stronger the wind**.

## The Coriolis effect

Because the Earth rotates, moving air is deflected: to the **right in the northern hemisphere** and to the left in the south. Aloft, where there is no friction, the balance between the pressure force and Coriolis makes the wind blow **parallel to the isobars** (the **geostrophic wind**). In the northern hemisphere:

- around a **low**, the wind turns **anticlockwise**;
- around a **high**, it turns **clockwise**.

In the southern hemisphere it is the other way round.

## Buys Ballot's law

In the northern hemisphere, **if you stand with your back to the wind, low pressure is on your left**. It is a quick way to know where the bad weather is. Remember too, from the instruments module, that flying towards low pressure without resetting makes the altimeter over-read.

## Friction

Near the ground, friction **slows** the wind. As it loses speed, Coriolis deflects it less and the wind **crosses the isobars towards low pressure**. That is why, climbing from the ground, the wind usually **increases** and **veers** (turns to the right, in the northern hemisphere). In practice: the wind at 2,000 ft can be quite different from the one the tower gives.

## Local winds

- **Sea breeze**: by day, the land warms more than the sea and the air blows from sea to land. At night it reverses (**land breeze**).
- **Valley and mountain breezes**: by day, air rises up the warmed slopes; at night, cold air drains down into the valley.
- Strong winds crossing mountains can produce <Term id="mountain-wave">mountain waves</Term> and turbulence downwind (lesson 5).

## The jet stream

The <Term id="jet-stream" /> is a narrow band of **very strong** winds near the tropopause, usually blowing from **west to east** in mid-latitudes. It is stronger in **winter**, when the temperature contrast between pole and equator is greater.

For long-haul flights it has two sides:

- as a **tailwind** it saves time and fuel: that is why flights from America to Europe are usually shorter than the return;
- at its edges there is strong shear and there may be <Term id="clear-air-turbulence" />.
