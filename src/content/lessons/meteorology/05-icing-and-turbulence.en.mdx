## When ice forms

Structural <Term id="icing" /> needs two things:

1. **Visible moisture**: cloud or precipitation.
2. The aircraft's surface at **0 °C or below**.

Clouds contain a lot of **supercooled** water: liquid droplets below 0 °C that freeze on hitting the aircraft. The risk is greatest at the temperatures where such droplets are common, from 0 °C to about −20 °C. Colder than that, clouds tend to be made of ice crystals, which do not stick as much.

## Types of ice

| Type | How it forms | Appearance and risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Rime** | **Small** droplets that freeze instantly | White, opaque and brittle; it distorts the leading edge |
| **Clear** | **Large** droplets that spread before freezing | Transparent, hard and heavy; hard to see and remove. The most dangerous |
| **Mixed** | A combination of both | Features of both |

<Term id="freezing-rain" /> builds clear ice very quickly. It usually occurs when there is a warm layer aloft over sub-zero air near the ground, for example ahead of a warm front in winter. Large supercooled drops can form ice **behind** the protected areas of the wing, beyond what the anti-ice systems cover.

## Icing intensities

The AIM defines four intensities for reporting icing:

| Intensity | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Trace** | Ice is perceptible but builds up slowly |
| **Light** | May be a problem if flown in for more than an hour without protection |
| **Moderate** | Even a short encounter is dangerous; systems must be used or the aircraft diverted |
| **Severe** | The systems cannot control the ice: exit immediately |

## Turbulence

<Term id="turbulence" /> by cause:

- **Convective**: thermals on warm days and under towering cloud.
- **Mechanical**: wind blowing over obstacles, buildings or rough terrain.
- **Mountain wave**: strong, stable wind crossing a mountain range. **Lenticular** clouds betray the waves, and under their crests there may be **rotors** with severe turbulence.
- **Clear-air** (<Term id="clear-air-turbulence">CAT</Term>): at altitude, with no cloud, linked to shear near the jet stream. It cannot be seen and weather radar does not detect it.
- **Wake**: other aircraft's vortices (Module 2).

## Turbulence intensities

| Intensity | What you feel |
|---|---|
| **Light** | Slight, momentary changes; seat belts felt a little |
| **Moderate** | Stronger changes, but the aircraft stays under control; walking in the cabin is difficult |
| **Severe** | Large, abrupt changes in altitude and attitude; momentary loss of control; loose objects thrown about |
| **Extreme** | The aircraft is tossed violently and is practically impossible to control; it may be damaged |

## What the crew does

- Sets the **turbulence penetration speed** given in the manual, holds the **attitude** and does not chase altitude.
- Switches on the **seat belt** sign and alerts the cabin crew. The FAA points out that most people injured by turbulence were not wearing their seat belts.
- **Reports** the turbulence and icing encountered (PIREP or AIREP), so that others can avoid them.
