## Air masses

An **air mass** is a large body of air with similar temperature and humidity, which it acquires in its source region:

- **maritime** (moist) or **continental** (dry);
- **polar** (cold) or **tropical** (warm).

As it moves, it changes according to the surface it passes over. For example, cold air moving over a warmer sea is heated from below and becomes **unstable**: showers and good visibility.

## Fronts

A **front** is the boundary between two different air masses.

### Cold front

At a <Term id="cold-front" />, cold air advances and wedges under the warm air. Its slope is **steep** and lifts the warm air forcefully:

- **towering** cloud and sometimes <Term id="cumulonimbus" /> or squall lines;
- heavy **showers** in a **narrow** band;
- a **sharp change** in wind and pressure as it passes;
- afterwards, colder air, often with **good visibility**.

### Warm front

At a <Term id="warm-front" />, warm air advances and rises **gently** over the cold air. Its slope is much gentler, so its cloud arrives hundreds of kilometres ahead:

- **cirrus** heralds its arrival, followed by cloud that gets **lower and thicker**;
- **steady precipitation** in a **wide** band;
- **low ceilings** and **poor visibility**;
- in winter there may be <Term id="freezing-rain" /> ahead of it: rain from the warm layer falls into the cold air below.

### Occluded front

When a cold front catches up with a warm front, the warm air is **lifted** off the ground: this is an **occlusion**. It combines features of both and can hide cumulonimbus within layered cloud.

## Fog

In METARs, **fog** (FG) is reported when visibility is **below 1,000 m**, and **mist** (BR) when it is between 1,000 and 5,000 m. Fog forms when air near the ground becomes **saturated**, through cooling or added moisture:

| Type | How it forms | When and where |
|---|---|---|
| **Radiation** | The ground cools at night and chills the air above it | **Clear** nights with **light wind** and moist air; it clears as the sun warms |
| **Advection** | Moist, mild air moving over a cold surface | Coasts and cold seas; it can form **with wind** and last for days |
| **Upslope** | Moist air rising up a slope and cooling | Windward slopes |
| **Steam** | Very cold air passing over warmer water | Lakes and rivers in winter |
| **Frontal** | Warm rain saturates the cold air beneath | Ahead of warm fronts |

<Callout type="tip" label="Warning sign">
A narrowing temperature–dew point spread in the evening, with clear skies and calm wind, points to possible radiation fog at dawn.
</Callout>
