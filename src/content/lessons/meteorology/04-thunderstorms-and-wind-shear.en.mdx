## The ingredients

A <Term id="thunderstorm" /> needs three things:

1. Enough **moisture**.
2. **Unstable** air.
3. A **lifting mechanism**: heating of the ground, a mountain, a front or converging wind.

## The three stages

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Cumulus** | **Updrafts** dominate; the cloud grows fast and no rain reaches the ground yet |
| **Mature** | **Precipitation reaches the ground** and **downdrafts** appear alongside the updrafts. It is the **most dangerous** stage |
| **Dissipating** | **Downdrafts** dominate; the storm weakens |

## The hazards

In and around a <Term id="cumulonimbus" /> you can meet all of these at once:

- severe or extreme **turbulence**, even far from the cloud;
- **hail**, which can also fall outside the cloud, under the anvil;
- severe **icing**;
- **lightning**;
- very heavy **rain**;
- **wind shear** and **microbursts** near the ground.

FAA Advisory Circular AC 00-24C warns that holding a constant altitude in a thunderstorm is almost impossible, and that turbulence may be met **20 or more miles** from the edge of the anvil.

## How far away

The classic FAA recommendation is to **avoid by at least 20 miles** any thunderstorm identified as severe or giving an intense radar echo, especially under the anvil of a large cumulonimbus. Also:

- **do not take off or land** with a thunderstorm approaching;
- **do not fly under** a thunderstorm, even if you can see through it;
- **do not try to pass** between two storms that are close together.

## The microburst

A <Term id="microburst" /> is a small, very intense downdraft. The FAA AIM gives these figures:

- the downdraft is usually **less than a mile** across and, near the ground, spreads out to about **2.5 miles**;
- it can reach **6,000 ft/min** downwards;
- horizontal winds near the ground can reach **45 kt**, which means a **90 kt** shear for an aircraft flying through it (from 45 kt headwind to 45 kt tailwind);
- a microburst seldom lasts **more than 15 minutes** from the time it reaches the ground, and its winds increase during the first 5.

### What happens to an aircraft on approach

1. On entry, it meets an increasing **headwind**: indicated airspeed **rises** and the aircraft tends to go **above** the glide path. The natural reaction is to reduce power, which makes what follows worse.
2. In the middle, the **downdraft** pushes the aircraft towards the ground.
3. On exit, the wind becomes a **tailwind**: indicated airspeed **drops** sharply and the aircraft loses lift close to the ground.

That is why, with a <Term id="wind-shear" /> warning, the escape manoeuvre is **maximum thrust** and a **nose-up attitude** up to the limit of stall protection, without changing configuration until clear of danger, following the manufacturer's procedure.
