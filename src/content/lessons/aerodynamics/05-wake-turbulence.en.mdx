## Where it comes from

<Term id="wake-turbulence">Wake turbulence</Term> is a direct consequence of lift. The pressure difference between the lower and upper surfaces makes air curl around the wingtips and form **two counter-rotating vortices** behind the aircraft. They are the same vortices responsible for <Term id="induced-drag" />.

An aircraft starts generating them when it **rotates** on take-off and stops when it **touches down**.

## When it is strongest

The wake is strongest when the generating aircraft is:

- **heavy**,
- in a **clean configuration** (flaps and other devices retracted), and
- **slow**.

All of these mean a higher angle of attack and stronger vortices. That is why the classic risk is following a large aircraft that has just taken off or is on approach.

## How it moves

- Vortices from large aircraft **sink** at several hundred feet per minute, weakening with time and distance. Atmospheric turbulence speeds up their breakup.
- **Close to the ground**, within about 100–200 ft, they drift sideways at about 2–3 kt.
- A light **crosswind** of 1 to 5 kt can slow the upwind vortex and leave it in the touchdown zone, while pushing the downwind vortex towards another runway. A tailwind can carry the preceding aircraft's wake forward into the touchdown zone.

## Wake categories and separation

To set minimum distances or times between aircraft, ICAO PANS-ATM classifies aircraft by their maximum certificated take-off mass:

| Category | Maximum take-off mass |
|---|---|
| **Light** (L) | 7,000 kg or less |
| **Medium** (M) | More than 7,000 kg and less than 136,000 kg |
| **Heavy** (H) | 136,000 kg or more |
| **Super** (J) | Specifically designated types, such as the Airbus A380 |

<Term id="atc">Air traffic control</Term> applies wake separation and warns with the phrase "caution, wake turbulence". Some airspace also uses more detailed category schemes.

## Basic avoidance techniques

- **Landing behind a larger aircraft:** stay at or above its approach path, note its touchdown point and land **beyond** it.
- **Landing after a larger aircraft has departed:** note its rotation point and land **well before** it.
- **Departing behind a larger aircraft:** rotate **before** its rotation point and climb above its flight path until clear of its wake.
