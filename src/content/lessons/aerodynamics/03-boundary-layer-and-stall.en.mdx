## The boundary layer

Right at the surface of the wing, friction slows the air down: in contact with the skin its speed is zero, and it increases to free-stream speed a few millimetres away. That thin region is the **boundary layer**.

- **Laminar:** the air moves in orderly layers. Little friction, but little energy, so it separates easily.
- **Turbulent:** the air mixes. More friction, but more energy, so it stays attached to the surface longer.

Over the upper surface, the air has to move towards rising pressure (towards the trailing edge). The higher the <Term id="angle-of-attack" />, the harder this becomes, until the boundary layer **separates**. **Vortex generators**, small vanes on the wing, mix fast-moving air into the boundary layer to delay that separation.

## How a stall develops

1. As angle of attack increases, the separation point moves forward from the trailing edge.
2. Beyond the critical angle of attack, separation covers much of the wing: lift drops and drag rises sharply.
3. On a well-designed wing the process starts at the root, so the ailerons stay effective for longer.

**Usual warnings** of a <Term id="stall" />: vibration or buffet as separated airflow strikes the tail, "mushy", less effective controls, and artificial warnings such as the stall horn on light aircraft or the stick shaker on transport aircraft.

## The spin

A **spin** is an aggravated stall in which the aircraft goes into **autorotation**: one wing is more deeply stalled than the other and the aircraft descends along a corkscrew path. Two things are needed: **stall plus yaw**. That is why an uncoordinated stall — for example, in a skidding turn at low speed — is especially dangerous.

## Ground effect

When the wing flies within about one wingspan of the ground, the surface restricts the wingtip vortices and the downwash. The result is <Term id="ground-effect" />:

- <Term id="induced-drag" /> falls and the aircraft tends to **float** in the flare, especially if it arrives too fast;
- an aircraft can **lift off** in ground effect at too low a speed and then be unable to climb once it leaves it, when induced drag returns to normal.
