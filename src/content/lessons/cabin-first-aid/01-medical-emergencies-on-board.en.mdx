<Callout type="official-procedure" label="The operator's manual prevails">
This lesson summarises general first-aid principles and does not replace an accredited hands-on course. On-board medical equipment, procedures and the limits of what crew may do are set by the operator.
</Callout>

## More common than you might think

A study published in *The New England Journal of Medicine* in 2013 analysed almost 12,000 medical emergencies on flights by five airlines. Its main figures:

| Figure | Value |
|---|---|
| Frequency | **1 emergency every 604 flights** |
| Most common problem | **Fainting or near-fainting** (37.4 %) |
| Next | **Respiratory** symptoms (12.1 %) and **nausea or vomiting** (9.5 %) |
| A doctor travelling as a passenger helped | 48.1 % |
| Aircraft diversion | 7.3 % |
| Deaths | 0.3 % |

In other words: over a cabin crew career, looking after a passenger who feels unwell is routine. It is almost always resolved on board, but you have to be ready for the serious cases.

## How the response is organised

1. **Safety**: make sure the area is safe for whoever helps.
2. **Assess** the passenger: are they responsive? Are they breathing normally?
3. **Call for help**: alert another crew member and the senior cabin crew member, who informs the commander.
4. **Look for a healthcare professional** among the passengers with an announcement. It is wise to check their qualification, and responsibility for the aircraft remains with the commander.
5. **Treat** with the on-board equipment, using gloves and other protection against body fluids.
6. **Record** what happened: symptoms, times, treatment and who gave it.

Many airlines also have a **ground-based medical advice** service that the flight deck can talk to by radio. The decision to divert to another airport always belongs to the **commander**, who takes it with the medical information, the state of the flight and the airports available.

## On-board equipment

| Equipment | For | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| **<Term id="first-aid-kit" />** | Wounds, burns, bandaging, minor problems | The crew |
| **Emergency medical kit** | More advanced medicines and instruments | A doctor or other qualified person |
| **First-aid oxygen** | Passengers who need it, for example after a decompression | The crew |
| **<Term id="aed" />** (if carried) | Cardiac arrest | Trained people |

In the EU, the number of first-aid kits depends on the seats: **one up to 100**, **two from 101 to 200** and so on, up to **six** above 500. Aircraft with more than 30 seats must also carry an **emergency medical kit** when any point on the route is more than **60 minutes** from an aerodrome with qualified medical assistance. The commander may restrict access to that kit if the situation calls for it.
