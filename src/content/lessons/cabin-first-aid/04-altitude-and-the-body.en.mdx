## The cabin is not at sea level

A pressurised aircraft keeps the cabin at an equivalent altitude of up to about **8,000 ft**. For a healthy person this is not a problem, but each breath contains less oxygen than on the ground, and cabin air is very **dry**. In passengers with heart or lung conditions, or after alcohol or lack of sleep, it can be noticeable.

## The four types of hypoxia

The FAA PHAK classifies <Term id="hypoxia" /> by its cause:

| Type | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypoxic** | Little oxygen available in the air | Altitude or a decompression |
| **Hypemic** | The blood cannot carry oxygen properly | Carbon monoxide, anaemia, recent blood donation |
| **Stagnant** | The blood does not circulate properly | Extreme cold, circulatory problems |
| **Histotoxic** | The cells cannot use the oxygen | Alcohol and some drugs |

The PHAK gives a striking figure: **one ounce (about 30 ml) of alcohol** can be equivalent to about **2,000 ft** of extra physiological altitude.

## Hyperventilation

Anxiety, fear or pain can make a person **breathe too fast** and blow off too much carbon dioxide. The symptoms (dizziness, tingling in the hands and lips, feeling short of breath) resemble those of hypoxia.

- **Talk** to the person calmly and ask them to **breathe more slowly**, for example by counting or talking out loud.
- If in doubt whether it is hypoxia, especially at high altitude or after a pressurisation problem, treat it **as hypoxia** and give oxygen.

## Ears and sinuses

During the **descent**, cabin pressure rises and air must get into the middle ear through the Eustachian tube. If it cannot (for example, with a cold), **ear pain** sets in and can be severe.

- Recommend **swallowing, yawning, chewing** or a gentle **Valsalva** manoeuvre: mouth closed, nose pinched and blow gently.
- Babies can be breastfed, bottle-fed or given a dummy during the descent.

The same can happen in the **sinuses**.

## Diving before flying

After scuba diving, the body has extra nitrogen dissolved in the blood. Flying too soon can cause **decompression sickness**. The FAA AIM recommends waiting:

- at least **12 hours** after a dive with no decompression stops, before flying up to 8,000 ft;
- at least **24 hours** after a dive with decompression stops, or before any flight above 8,000 ft.

A passenger with joint pain, tingling or difficulty breathing who has dived that same day needs **oxygen** and medical attention.

## Dehydration

Dry cabin air, heat and drinks such as coffee or alcohol encourage **dehydration**: headache, tiredness, cramps, dizziness. Offering water regularly, especially on long flights, is a simple preventive measure.
