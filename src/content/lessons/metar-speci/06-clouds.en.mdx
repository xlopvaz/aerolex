## Amount: oktas

The sky is imagined as divided into **eight parts (oktas)**. The amount of each layer is coded as:

| Code | Meaning | Coverage |
|---|---|---|
| `FEW` | Few | 1–2 oktas |
| `SCT` | Scattered | 3–4 oktas |
| `BKN` | Broken | 5–7 oktas |
| `OVC` | Overcast | 8 oktas |

## Height of the base

Three digits in **hundreds of feet** above aerodrome elevation:

- `SCT030` → 3,000 ft.
- `BKN008` → 800 ft.
- `OVC120` → 12,000 ft.

## Type: CB and TCU only

Only two cloud types are reported, because they are hazardous: `CB` (**cumulonimbus**, the thunderstorm cloud) and `TCU` (**towering cumulus**).

<MetarBreakdown parts={[
  ["FEW010", "Few clouds at 1,000 ft"],
  ["SCT025CB", "Scattered cumulonimbus at 2,500 ft"],
  ["BKN040", "Broken layer (5–7 oktas) at 4,000 ft"]
]} />

Layers are listed **from the bottom up**. As a rule, the lowest layer is reported even if it is few, then the next layer covering more than 2 oktas, then the next covering more than 4 oktas, plus any CB or TCU.

## Ceiling

The <Term id="ceiling" /> is the height of the base of the lowest **BKN or OVC** layer (below 20,000 ft). In the example above, the ceiling is 4,000 ft. It is key for <Term id="vfr" /> flight and for approach minima.

## When there is no cloud to report

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `NSC` | No significant cloud: none below 5,000 ft (or the highest minimum sector altitude) and no CB or TCU |
| `NCD` | No cloud detected (automatic stations) |
| `SKC` | Sky clear (used by some States) |
| `CLR` | US automatic stations: no cloud detected below 12,000 ft |
| `VV002` | Sky obscured (e.g. by fog): vertical visibility 200 ft |

## CAVOK

<Term id="cavok" /> replaces visibility, present weather and cloud all at once when visibility is 10 km or more, there is no significant cloud and no CB/TCU, and no significant weather. It does **not** mean a completely clear sky.

<Callout type="caution" label="Caution">
METAR heights are **above the aerodrome**, not above sea level. At high-elevation airports the difference matters when comparing them with altitudes on charts.
</Callout>
