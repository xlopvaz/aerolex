## The basic group: `dddffKT`

- `ddd`: direction the wind is blowing **from**, in degrees true, rounded to the nearest ten.
- `ff`: mean speed.
- `KT`: unit, knots. Some countries use metres per second (`MPS`).

`24012KT` means wind **from** 240° (south-west) at 12 knots. Careful: a 240° wind comes from the south-west and pushes towards the north-east.

Under ICAO rules, direction and speed are **averages over the 10 minutes** before the observation.

## Gusts: `G`

When gusts exceed the mean speed by **10 knots or more**, they are added with a `G`:

## Direction variation: `dddVddd`

If the direction varies by **60° or more but less than 180°** and the mean speed is **3 knots or more**, a group with the two extremes is added, such as `350V060`.

## Variable wind: `VRB`

`VRB` replaces the direction when:

- the direction varies by 60° or more and the mean speed is **less than 3 knots** (`VRB02KT`), or
- the variation is **180° or more**, or a single direction cannot be determined (for example, with a thunderstorm over the aerodrome).

## Calm: `00000KT`

When there is no wind, the group is `00000KT`.

<Callout type="caution" label="Caution: true vs magnetic">
In a METAR the wind is given relative to **true north**. The wind given by the tower or the <Term id="atis" /> for take-off and landing, however, is relative to **magnetic north**, like the numbering of the <Term id="runway" />. In Spain the difference is currently small, but in other parts of the world it can be large.
</Callout>

## Why does it matter so much?

Aircraft take off and land **into the wind** whenever they can, because they then need less runway. Each aircraft also has a <Term id="crosswind" /> limit that must not be exceeded. With the METAR wind and the runway orientation, the pilot estimates those components.

<Callout type="regional-diff" label="ICAO / US">
In US METARs the mean wind is averaged over **2 minutes**, and `VRB` is used when the direction is variable with a speed of **6 knots or less**.
</Callout>
