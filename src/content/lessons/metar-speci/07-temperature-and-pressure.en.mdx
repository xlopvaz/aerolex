## Temperature and dew point: `TT/TdTd`

Two numbers in **whole degrees Celsius** separated by a slash: first the air temperature, then the <Term id="dew-point" />. Sub-zero values are preceded by an `M` (*minus*).

## The spread matters

The **closer** temperature and dew point are, the closer the air is to saturation. A spread of only a few degrees, especially around dusk with clear skies and light wind, points to possible **fog or low cloud**.

Temperature also affects **performance**: in hot weather the air is less dense, engines produce less thrust and the aircraft needs more runway to take off.

## Pressure: QNH

The last mandatory group is the <Term id="qnh" />, the pressure to set on the <Term id="altimeter" /> so that it reads altitude above mean sea level.

- `Q1013` → 1013 hPa. Under ICAO rules it is rounded **down** to the whole hectopascal.
- `Q0998` → 998 hPa (a leading zero keeps four digits).

<Callout type="regional-diff" label="ICAO / US">
In the US the setting is given in **inches of mercury**, in hundredths: `A2992` = 29.92 inHg, roughly 1013 hPa (the standard atmosphere).
</Callout>

## Why set it correctly?

The altimeter measures pressure, not distance to the ground. If you fly from high pressure to low pressure without updating QNH, the altimeter **reads higher than you really are**: you are lower than you think. Hence the classic rule: *"from high to low, look out below"*. Near sea level, each hectopascal is worth roughly 27–30 ft.

<Callout type="info" label="QNH and QFE">
With <Term id="qfe" /> set, the altimeter reads height above the aerodrome (zero on the runway). The METAR gives QNH; QFE comes from other sources, such as the ATIS or the tower, where it is used.
</Callout>
